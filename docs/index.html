Nepal cryosphere inventory

Every mapped glacier and glacial lake in Nepal, joined into one dataset: 4,480 glaciers holding 4,215 km² of ice, 1,429 lakes covering 81 km², annual ice speed to 2025, and every recorded outburst flood. Open data, open pipeline, and explicit about what it cannot tell you.

The two inventories, side by side

Ice and water occupy different elevations. Glacier area peaks between 5,000 and 6,200 m; lakes cluster about a thousand metres lower, on and beyond the debris-covered tongues. The gap between the two curves is where outburst floods start.

Lakes touching ice are the ones growing

Share of lakes that expanded between 2016–17 and 2022–24, grouped by distance to the nearest mapped glacier. Lakes at the ice margin expand around three times as often as detached ones, and the gradient is monotonic. Across all assessable lakes the net area gain is , close to the 5.5% the source study reports for High Mountain Asia as a whole.

Expansion means a gain above both 0.005 km² and the source study's own area uncertainty. Lakes under 0.01 km², or with fewer than three cloud-free images in either period, are not assessed.

Twenty-three years of retreat, made visible

lakes now lie inside outlines that RGI still maps as ice

RGI 7.0's Nepal outlines come from imagery with a median date of 1999. The lakes are from 2022. So lakes — km² of open water across 163 glaciers — fall inside polygons the glacier inventory still calls glacier. Hi-MAG independently classifies 98 of them as proglacial and only 16 as supraglacial, so these are overwhelmingly not ponds on the ice surface. They are lakes filling ground the terminus has vacated.

This cuts both ways. It is a real, measurable retreat signal. It also means every ice-contact judgement in this inventory is made against a 1999 margin and will overstate contact for glaciers that have since pulled back. The field is named dist_to_rgi1999_m so the gap cannot be forgotten downstream.

What this data cannot tell you

There is no usable ice-velocity trend here

Comparing glacier speed in 2000–2009 against 2016–2024 gives a median slowdown of about 46%, with 96% of glaciers apparently slowing. It is an artefact and should not be published.

Speed is the hypotenuse of two velocity components, so it cannot go below zero and random error pushes it upward. Early years rest on a handful of satellite image pairs; recent years rest on thousands. As the error collapsed, apparent speed fell with it. Median signal-to-noise across Nepal is in the 2000s against today — the older velocities sit at the noise floor.

Filtering for high early signal-to-noise makes it worse, not better: it selects the glaciers noise happened to inflate, so regression to the mean reproduces the same slowdown. velocity_change_interpretable is set to false for every glacier.

This is a statement about this dataset processed this way, not about Himalayan glaciers. Published work on High Mountain Asia velocity trends has not been verified here; check it independently.

Do lakes make their glaciers flow faster?

Elsewhere, lake-terminating glaciers often flow faster than land-terminating ones. This data does not show that for Nepal. Raw medians suggest contact-lake glaciers are faster — 2.11 against 1.81 m/yr — but that reverses once you compare like with like, because glaciers with lakes are systematically larger.

Read this as an absence of evidence, not evidence of absence: samples are small, Nepal's glaciers are slow and heavily debris-covered, and a glacier-wide mean speed is a blunt instrument for a terminus process.

Read this before using the data

Outlines are two decades old

RGI 7.0 source imagery for Nepal spans 1992–2010, median 1999. Sampling 2024 velocity inside a 1999 outline includes ground that has since deglaciated, biasing glacier-wide means low. The inventory is not a current map of ice extent.

Two definitions of “in Nepal”

4,679 glaciers intersect the border; 4,480 have their representative point inside it. Headline figures use the second. The first pulls in Tibetan glaciers such as Rongbuk that are under 2% inside Nepal. Both are in the files, with frac_within_nepal.

The border itself is contested

The Kalapani–Limpiyadhura area in the northwest is disputed between Nepal and India. This build follows the geoBoundaries gbOpen ADM0 line; a different boundary changes far-western counts.

This is not a hazard ranking

There is no danger score, deliberately. Judging whether a lake is likely to burst needs moraine dam geometry, freeboard, ice content in the dam, slope stability above the lake, and downstream exposure. None of that is in these sources. The inventory supplies the objective ingredients and stops. A composite index built only from area, growth and ice contact would look authoritative and mean very little.

Small lakes are under-counted

The lake source reports detection near 96% for lakes of 20,000–100,000 m² and 100% above that, but roughly 25% below 5,400 m². Small lakes have caused destructive floods, so do not read the count of tiny lakes as complete.

RGI cannot identify lake-terminating glaciers

Every glacier in RGI region 15 carries term_type = 9, unassigned. That gap is why the lake inventory exists: matching lakes back to outlines supplies the flag RGI does not.
